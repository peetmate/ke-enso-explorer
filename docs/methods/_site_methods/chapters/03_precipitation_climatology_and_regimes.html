4  Precipitation Climatology, Empirical Quantiles & Regimes

5 Precipitation Climatology, Empirical Quantiles & Regimes

This chapter defines the non-parametric empirical quantile baselines (\(P_{33}, P_{67}, P_{10}, P_{90}\)) over the 1991–2020 WMO standard normal period. It provides mathematical rationale for treating extreme positive rainfall skewness without Gaussian distortion, and formalizes independent seasonal standardization for consecutive sequence classification.

5.1 Figure 3.1: Multi-Decadal Precipitation Climatology & Empirical Tercile Bounds

Section in Platform: Section 3  |  Target Identifier: fig_2_1  |  Output Type: Chart

Policy / Scientific Question: How unusual was seasonal rainfall or drought across the historical satellite record, and where do this season’s totals sit against the empirical tercile and extreme-decile bounds of the 1991–2020 normal?

5.1.1 Scientific Purpose & Context

Kenya’s rainfall is bimodal. The Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ) crosses the equator twice a year, and each passage drives a rainy season: the March to May Long Rains (MAM) on the northward crossing and the October to December Short Rains (OND) on the southward crossing. The two seasons are not interchangeable. MAM delivers the larger share of annual rainfall in the western and central highlands and underpins the main maize harvest, whereas OND dominates the eastern and northern arid and semi-arid lands (ASAL) and is the season most strongly modulated by the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD). Between the two sits the long dry season, during which pastoral systems depend entirely on the pasture and water recharge delivered by the preceding rains.

Figure 3.1 places every season since 1981 for the selected county against this climatology. Bars show the county-mean seasonal total from the Climate Hazards Center InfraRed Precipitation with Station data (CHIRPS v3), the 1991–2020 WMO standard normal mean is drawn as a reference line, and four dashed horizontal rules mark the empirical tercile bounds (P33 and P67) and extreme deciles (P10 and P90) of the same 30-year baseline. A strip beneath the x-axis colours each year by the concurrent ocean driver phase (ENSO and IOD for OND; the Western-V index for MAM) so that the reader can see whether an anomalous season coincided with a known teleconnection state. The anomaly view re-expresses the bars as departures from the normal in millimetres and in percent, and the SPEI-3 option substitutes the three-month Standardized Precipitation-Evapotranspiration Index so that evaporative demand is accounted for.

The figure exists because the rainfall regime, not the annual mean, determines livelihood risk. Pastoral and marginal rainfed systems in the ASAL counties operate close to the lower tercile in most years. A season that falls at or below P33 removes the margin that herds and household food stocks rely on, and a season at or below P10 is the kind of event that triggers the National Drought Management Authority (NDMA) alarm phases and humanitarian scale-up. Expressing each season as a position within the empirical distribution of its own county and season gives county planners, the Kenya Meteorological Department (KMD) and humanitarian partners a single, comparable language for how unusual a season was.

5.1.2 Mathematical Formulation & Algorithmic Approach

Baseline Reference Period: 1991–2020 WMO standard normal (N = 30 seasons per county and season)

Data chain. CHIRPS v3.0 monthly precipitation at 0.05 degree resolution is summed per pixel into the twelve rolling three-month windows (JFM to DJF) and averaged over the pixels inside each county polygon (value_mean; the within-county pixel standard deviation is retained as value_sd). For the selected county, season and variable the figure queries one value per year from 1981 onward. The climatological baseline is the subset of years 1991 to 2020 inclusive, giving N = 30 seasonal totals per county and season.

Empirical quantile estimator. All tercile and decile bounds are sample quantiles computed with the Hyndman and Fan (1996) Type 7 definition, the default in R (quantile(type = 7)), NumPy (method = "linear"), DuckDB (quantile_cont) and D3 (d3.quantile, used in the browser). The N baseline values are sorted ascending, X(1) <= X(2) <= … <= X(N). For a probability p the estimator sets h = (N - 1)p + 1, takes the integer part j = floor(h) and the fractional part gamma = h - j, and interpolates linearly between the j-th and (j+1)-th order statistics. The estimator is continuous in p, uses only order statistics, and makes no assumption about the shape of the distribution. With N = 30 the four bounds used by the figure fall at h = 3.9 (P10, between the 3rd and 4th smallest years), h = 10.67 (P33), h = 20.33 (P67) and h = 27.1 (P90). Bounds are rounded to whole millimetres for display only; classification uses the unrounded values.

Classification. Each season is assigned one of five regime labels by comparison with the bounds: Much drier (R <= P10), Drier (P10 < R <= P33), Near normal (P33 < R < P67), Wetter (P67 <= R < P90) and Much wetter (R >= P90). The three-way tercile collapse used elsewhere in the platform (Drier / Near normal / Wetter) is the same rule with the decile bounds dropped. The anomaly view reports R - mean(1991–2020) in millimetres and as a percentage of the normal; no standardization by the standard deviation is applied to rainfall in this figure.

SPEI-3 option. When the user selects SPEI-3 the bars show the index value directly. SPEI is already a standardized quantity, produced upstream by fitting a log-logistic distribution to the three-month climatic water balance (precipitation minus reference evapotranspiration) and mapping it to a standard normal variate, so no further tercile computation is applied; the conventional drought categories of the index are used instead.

5.1.2.1 Methodological Rationale

Why empirical quantiles rather than Gaussian bounds. The tercile convention used by many seasonal forecasting centres, mu plus or minus 0.43 sigma, is exact only when seasonal rainfall is normally distributed, because Phi(-0.43) = 0.333. East African seasonal rainfall is not normally distributed. Rainfall is bounded below by zero and generated by a small number of convective events, so the distribution has a long right tail. Computed from the CHIRPS county series over 1991–2020, the sample skewness coefficient of OND totals has a median of 1.23 across Kenya’s 47 counties (interquartile range 0.95 to 1.65), exceeds 1.0 in 33 counties and reaches 3.0 in Isiolo and Wajir; no county has negative OND skew. MAM is less skewed (median 0.82) but still positive in 45 of 48 units. Coefficients of variation reach 0.90 for OND in the northeastern counties.

Consequences of imposing symmetry. For a right-skewed variable the mean exceeds the median and the standard deviation is inflated by the tail, so both symmetric bounds are displaced upward relative to the true tercile cut points. The lower bound mu - 0.43 sigma sits above the empirical P33 (median displacement +4 percent of P33 for OND across counties, up to +31 percent in the most skewed counties), so more than one third of baseline years fall below it: the Gaussian rule labels a median 36.7 percent of 1991–2020 OND seasons as dry where by construction P33 labels exactly one third. The error is in the direction of over-diagnosing drought and is largest precisely in the ASAL counties where drought response is most costly. The upper bound mu + 0.43 sigma is displaced further still (median +16 percent above the empirical P67 for OND, +52 percent in Wajir), so only about 23 percent of baseline years exceed it instead of one third: the symmetric rule under-counts wet seasons and overstates the rarity of the convective deluges that drive flood and Rift Valley fever risk. At the extremes the Gaussian rule becomes physically meaningless: mu - 1.28 sigma, the parametric P10, is negative whenever the coefficient of variation exceeds 0.78, which occurs in the northeastern counties, so no observed season could ever be classified as “much drier”.

Illustration with a lognormal model. If seasonal totals follow a lognormal law with coefficient of variation 0.5, the true tercile bounds are 0.73 mu and 1.10 mu and the true P10 is 0.49 mu, whereas the Gaussian rule gives 0.79 mu, 1.22 mu and 0.36 mu. The mismatch is a property of skewness, not of sampling noise, and does not shrink as the record lengthens. The empirical Type 7 estimator avoids it by never leaving the observed order statistics, at the cost of larger sampling variance for the decile bounds (see limitations). Because the same estimator is applied in the browser, in the DuckDB queries and in the Python build scripts, tercile labels are reproducible across the platform.

Governing Mathematical Formulations:

Hyndman and Fan Type 7 sample quantile. X(1) <= … <= X(N) are the sorted 1991–2020 seasonal totals for one county and season (N = 30); the estimator interpolates linearly between adjacent order statistics.

\[ \hat{Q}(p) = (1 - \gamma)\, X_{(j)} + \gamma\, X_{(j+1)}, \qquad h = (N - 1)\,p + 1, \quad j = \lfloor h \rfloor, \quad \gamma = h - j \]

Empirical tercile bounds over the 1991–2020 normal. Drier than normal is R <= T33, wetter than normal is R >= T67; by construction each tercile holds one third of the baseline years.

\[ T_{33} = \hat{Q}_{1991\text{--}2020}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right), \qquad T_{67} = \hat{Q}_{1991\text{--}2020}\!\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right) \]

Extreme decile bounds. Much drier is R <= D10 and much wetter is R >= D90; with N = 30 these bounds rest on the 3rd/4th and 27th/28th order statistics.

\[ D_{10} = \hat{Q}_{1991\text{--}2020}(0.10), \qquad D_{90} = \hat{Q}_{1991\text{--}2020}(0.90) \]

Five-class regime assignment. R is the county-mean seasonal total; the three-way tercile labels used elsewhere drop the decile splits.

\[ \text{Regime}(R) = \begin{cases} \text{Much drier} & R \le D_{10} \\ \text{Drier} & D_{10} < R \le T_{33} \\ \text{Near normal} & T_{33} < R < T_{67} \\ \text{Wetter} & T_{67} \le R < D_{90} \\ \text{Much wetter} & R \ge D_{90} \end{cases} \]

Sample skewness coefficient (Fisher-Pearson). Positive values indicate a long right tail; OND county series have median gamma1 = 1.23 over 1991–2020, which is why symmetric Gaussian bounds are rejected.

\[ \gamma_1 = \frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(R_i - \bar{R}\right)^3}{\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(R_i - \bar{R}\right)^2\right]^{3/2}} \]

Gaussian tercile bounds (shown for contrast, not used). Both symmetric bounds are displaced upward under positive skew, over-diagnosing dry seasons and under-counting wet ones.

\[ \mu \pm 0.43\,\sigma \quad\text{since}\quad \Phi(-0.43) \approx 0.333; \qquad \mu - 0.43\sigma > T_{33} \text{ and } \mu + 0.43\sigma > T_{67} \text{ whenever } \gamma_1 > 0 \]

Anomaly view. Departures are expressed in millimetres and percent of the normal; rainfall is never divided by its standard deviation in this figure.

\[ A_y = R_y - \bar{R}_{1991\text{--}2020}, \qquad A_y^{\%} = 100\,\frac{R_y - \bar{R}_{1991\text{--}2020}}{\bar{R}_{1991\text{--}2020}} \]

5.1.4 Methodological Assumptions & Analytical Limitations

  • CHIRPS blends thermal infrared cold cloud duration estimates with station observations. Station density in northern and northeastern Kenya is sparse, so county means for the arid rangelands rely more heavily on the satellite component and on the CHPclim climatology than county means in the highlands; absolute totals carry larger uncertainty there than the rank-based tercile labels do.
  • The 0.05 degree (about 5.5 km) grid is averaged to a single county value. Strong orographic gradients inside large counties (for example the Marsabit and Nyambene massifs against the surrounding lowlands) are collapsed into one number; the within-county pixel standard deviation is stored but not used in classification.
  • Seasons are fixed calendar-month windows (MAM, OND and the other ten rolling windows). Late onset or early cessation that shifts rain across a window boundary is not captured, and the seasonal total says nothing about the dry-spell structure within the season.
  • The baseline holds only 30 values per county and season. Tercile bounds estimated from 30 observations have appreciable sampling error, and the decile bounds rest on only three observations in each tail; small differences between a season and a bound should not be over-interpreted.
  • The 1991–2020 normal is treated as stationary. The observed drying of MAM since the 1980s alongside a wetting tendency in OND means that the baseline mixes regimes; tercile frequencies computed over the full 1981 to present record will not be exactly one third.
  • Seasons are labelled by the calendar year in which the window ends (DJF 2020 spans December 2019 to February 2020). Windows the record cannot cover in full are absent.
  • SPEI-3 is standardized upstream with a fitted log-logistic distribution rather than with the empirical quantiles used for rainfall, so the two variables are not category-for-category comparable.
  • County aggregations rely on official polygon boundaries from the UN OCHA Common Operational Datasets (COD-AB); historical boundary changes are not back-cast.

5.2 Figure 3.1B: Consecutive Seasonal Sequences & Multi-Season Drought Persistence

Section in Platform: Section 3  |  Target Identifier: fig_2_1b  |  Output Type: Chart

Policy / Scientific Question: Did this county’s two rainy seasons fail back to back, how often has that happened before, how long did unbroken dry runs last, and what happened to maize yields when they did?

5.2.1 Scientific Purpose & Context

Drought impacts in Kenya compound across seasons rather than arriving in one. A single below-normal season stresses pasture and delays planting, but households, herds and shallow aquifers usually carry enough buffer to reach the next rains. Two consecutive failed seasons remove that buffer: dry-season grazing reserves are exhausted before the next rains, groundwater and pan recharge is skipped, breeding females are sold or die so the herd loses its reproductive core, seed stocks are eaten, and food prices rise while livestock prices collapse. The 2020 to 2023 drought, in which five successive seasons from OND 2020 to OND 2022 fell below normal across the ASAL counties, left more than four million people in Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) Phase 3 or worse by early 2023 according to the Kenya Food Security Steering Group short rains assessment. Operationally this is why NDMA drought phases and humanitarian early action protocols key on the sequence of seasons rather than on the latest one alone.

Figure 3.1B makes the sequence explicit. It pairs each production year Y with the two seasons that feed it, the Short Rains of the preceding calendar year (OND, Y−1) and the Long Rains of the same year (MAM, Y), and classifies the pair as Dry/Dry, Dry/Normal, Dry/Wet, Normal/Dry and so on. The OND(Y−1) → MAM(Y) pairing is used because those two seasons bracket the long dry season and jointly determine the pasture, water and planting conditions that the Y harvest and the Y livestock year depend on. A domino strip shows the pair state per production year, bars show the unbroken run length of consecutive dry seasons at the close of each year, and an outcome overlay shows county maize yield from HarvestStat and KNBS production so that the reader can see what happened to production in double-failure years relative to all years. Summary cards report the number and share of double failures in the record, the longest dry run with its total rainfall deficit, and the mean yield shortfall in double-failure years.

The analysis is descriptive and historical. It does not forecast the next season; its purpose is to give planners the empirical base rate of consecutive failure for their county, so that a dry OND can be read against how often a dry MAM has followed, and so that run-length thresholds in contingency plans are grounded in the county’s own record.

5.2.2 Mathematical Formulation & Algorithmic Approach

Baseline Reference Period: 1991–2020 WMO standard normal, applied separately to OND and to MAM (full-record baseline available as a toggle)

Inputs. For the selected county the module queries the county-mean seasonal total for every OND and MAM season from 1981 onward. The baseline toggle selects either the 1991–2020 WMO standard normal (default) or the full record; the formulation below is written for the default.

Independent seasonal standardization. The sample mean and sample standard deviation (N − 1 denominator) are computed separately for OND and for MAM from the baseline years, giving two distinct parameter pairs (mu_OND, sigma_OND) and (mu_MAM, sigma_MAM). Each season’s total is converted to a z-score using the parameters of its own season only. The two seasons are never pooled: OND and MAM differ in mean by a factor that ranges from well under one in the western highlands to well over two in the northeast, and their standard deviations differ correspondingly, so a pooled z would encode the seasonal cycle rather than the anomaly (see rationale).

State classification. A season is Dry when z < −0.5, Wet when z > +0.5 and Normal otherwise. The boundary values z = ±0.5 exactly fall in the Normal class. Seasons with no data are flagged missing and excluded from pairs. The anomaly in millimetres and in percent of the seasonal mean is carried alongside the z-score for display.

Chronological run length. All seasons are ordered MAM(1981), OND(1981), MAM(1982), OND(1982), and so on. Walking this sequence, a counter increments on every Dry season and resets to zero on any Normal, Wet or missing season; the counter value stored on each Dry season is its run length, the number of consecutive Dry seasons ending with it. The longest run in the record, the seasons it spans and its cumulative rainfall deficit relative to the sum of the seasonal normals are reported.

Production-year pairing. For each production year Y from the second year of the selected range, the pair is (State(OND, Y−1), State(MAM, Y)). A double failure is the Dry/Dry pair. The run length attached to year Y is the run at MAM(Y) if MAM(Y) is Dry, otherwise the run at OND(Y−1) if that season is Dry, otherwise zero; a run that was alive at OND(Y−1) but broken by a non-Dry MAM(Y) is flagged as broken. The ENSO phase and standardized Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI) for OND(Y−1), the IOD phase for OND(Y−1), and the RONI and Western-V phase for MAM(Y) are attached for the ocean-driver overlay.

Outcome overlay. County maize yield (t/ha, HarvestStat annual season) and production (HarvestStat and KNBS NAPR) are joined on harvest year Y. The summary compares the mean yield in double-failure years with the mean over all years with yield data and reports the percentage shortfall.

5.2.2.1 Methodological Rationale

Why the seasons must be standardized independently. Suppose the two seasons were pooled and a single mean and standard deviation computed from all 60 baseline season totals. Writing mu_O, mu_M for the seasonal means and sigma_O, sigma_M for the seasonal standard deviations, the pooled mean is (mu_O + mu_M)/2 and the pooled variance is the average of the two within-season variances plus a between-season term (mu_O − mu_M)^2/4. A season that is exactly normal for its own climatology, R = mu_s, would receive a pooled z-score of ±(mu_O − mu_M)/(2 sigma_pool), which is non-zero whenever the seasons differ in mean. In a county where OND averages 200 mm and MAM 450 mm, every ordinary OND would be classified Dry and every ordinary MAM Wet, and the “sequence” would simply reproduce the annual cycle. The between-season term also inflates sigma_pool, so genuine anomalies within either season would be compressed toward zero and under-detected. Standardizing each season against its own 1991–2020 moments removes both effects: a z of −0.5 means the same thing, half a within-season standard deviation below that season’s own normal, whether it occurs in OND or MAM, which is the comparability a sequence analysis requires.

Why z-scores rather than empirical terciles here. Elsewhere in the platform rainfall regimes are defined by empirical quantiles because seasonal rainfall is positively skewed. This module is the one place where rainfall is expressed as a z-score, and it is done deliberately: the sequence and run-length logic needs a single anomaly scale that is comparable across two seasons with different distributions, and a symmetric threshold of half a standard deviation is the same convention used for the ocean driver indices (RONI, DMI) that are overlaid on the strip. The consequence of the skew is accepted and reported rather than hidden. Under positive skew the share of seasons below z = −0.5 is somewhat higher than the 31 percent a Gaussian would give, and it varies by county with the degree of skew, so the module reports double-failure and run-length frequencies as empirical counts (X of N production years) rather than as nominal probabilities, and it never converts the z-scores to tercile labels.

Why the production-year pairing. Crop and livestock outcomes in year Y are driven by OND(Y−1) and MAM(Y) jointly: OND(Y−1) sets the dry-season pasture and water reserve and the residual soil moisture that MAM(Y) planting starts from, and MAM(Y) is the main cropping season in most counties. Pairing OND(Y) with MAM(Y) would split the dry season across two production years and would align the Y harvest with rains that fall after it.

Governing Mathematical Formulations:

Independent seasonal moments over the baseline. Each season has its own mean and sample standard deviation; the two seasons are never pooled.

\[ \mu_s = \frac{1}{N_s}\sum_{y \in B} R_{s,y}, \qquad \sigma_s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N_s - 1}\sum_{y \in B}\left(R_{s,y} - \mu_s\right)^2}, \qquad s \in \{\text{OND}, \text{MAM}\},\ B = 1991\text{--}2020 \]

Independent seasonal z-scores. A z of −0.5 is half a within-season standard deviation below that season’s own 1991–2020 normal, in either season.

\[ z_{\text{OND},y} = \frac{R_{\text{OND},y} - \mu_{\text{OND}}}{\sigma_{\text{OND}}}, \qquad z_{\text{MAM},y} = \frac{R_{\text{MAM},y} - \mu_{\text{MAM}}}{\sigma_{\text{MAM}}} \]

Pooled standardization bias (why pooling is rejected). A perfectly normal season would receive a non-zero pooled z-score, and the between-season term compresses true anomalies.

\[ \sigma_{\text{pool}}^2 = \frac{\sigma_{\text{OND}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{MAM}}^2}{2} + \frac{\left(\mu_{\text{OND}} - \mu_{\text{MAM}}\right)^2}{4}, \qquad z_{\text{pool}}\big|_{R = \mu_s} = \pm\frac{\mu_{\text{OND}} - \mu_{\text{MAM}}}{2\,\sigma_{\text{pool}}} \neq 0 \]

Seasonal state assignment. Strict inequalities; the boundary values are Normal. Missing seasons are excluded from pairs and reset the run counter.

\[ \text{State}(s, y) = \begin{cases} \text{Dry} & z_{s,y} < -0.5 \\ \text{Wet} & z_{s,y} > +0.5 \\ \text{Normal} & -0.5 \le z_{s,y} \le +0.5 \end{cases} \]

Chronological dry-run length. The run length is the number of consecutive Dry seasons ending at t; any non-Dry season resets it to zero.

\[ L_t = \left(L_{t-1} + 1\right)\,\mathbb{I}\!\left(z_t < -0.5\right), \qquad L_0 = 0, \qquad t \text{ indexes } \ldots, \text{MAM}_y, \text{OND}_y, \text{MAM}_{y+1}, \ldots \]

Production-year pair and double failure. Production year Y is fed by the preceding Short Rains and the same-year Long Rains; the nine possible pair states collapse to the four highlighted sequences Dry/Dry, Dry/Wet, Wet/Dry and Wet/Wet plus the Normal-containing pairs.

\[ \mathcal{P}_Y = \left(\text{State}(\text{OND}, Y-1),\ \text{State}(\text{MAM}, Y)\right), \qquad \text{DF}_Y = \mathbb{I}\!\left(\mathcal{P}_Y = (\text{Dry}, \text{Dry})\right) \]

Empirical double-failure frequency and yield shortfall. Frequencies are reported as counts of production years, and the yield comparison uses HarvestStat county maize yield in double-failure years against all years with data.

\[ f_{\text{DF}} = \frac{\sum_Y \text{DF}_Y}{\#\{Y : \mathcal{P}_Y \text{ complete}\}}, \qquad \Delta Y_{\text{DF}}^{\%} = 100\,\frac{\overline{Y}_{\text{maize}\mid \text{DF}} - \overline{Y}_{\text{maize}}}{\overline{Y}_{\text{maize}}} \]

5.2.4 Methodological Assumptions & Analytical Limitations

  • Seasons are fixed calendar windows. A Short Rains season that starts late and runs into January, or a Long Rains season that fails in April but recovers in June, is scored on the window total only, so sequence states can differ from the agronomic experience of the season.
  • The z-score threshold of ±0.5 is applied to a positively skewed variable. The share of seasons classed Dry is therefore not exactly the Gaussian 31 percent and differs between counties and between OND and MAM; comparisons across counties should use the empirical counts shown, not the nominal threshold.
  • Pasture and water respond to rainfall with a lag, and herd condition integrates over several seasons. A Dry/Dry pair is a rainfall statement; the livelihood consequence depends on the state the system entered the pair in, which the figure shows only through the preceding run length.
  • Consecutive seasons are treated as separate observations for classification, but OND and the following MAM are not statistically independent (they share ENSO and IOD persistence and soil moisture memory). The module does not model this dependence; the double-failure frequency it reports is the observed joint frequency, not a product of marginals.
  • The 1991–2020 baseline gives 30 values per season; the default z-scores for the 1980s and for years after 2020 are measured against a baseline they do not belong to. The full-record toggle trades this for a baseline that drifts as years are added.
  • The outcome overlay joins maize yield on harvest year. HarvestStat county series are incomplete for 2002 to 2014 and KNBS NAPR starts in 2019, so the yield-shortfall statistic rests on a subset of double-failure years and on maize only; pastoral outcomes (mortality, livestock prices) are not in the overlay.
  • Yields are not detrended in this module, so the shortfall in early double-failure years is measured against a long-run mean that includes later, higher-yielding years.
  • County aggregations rely on official polygon boundaries from the UN OCHA Common Operational Datasets (COD-AB).

5.3 Figure 3.2: Empirical Contingency Probability Matrix: Rainfall Terciles Conditioned on Ocean Driver Phase

Section in Platform: Section 3  |  Target Identifier: fig_2_2  |  Output Type: Chart

Policy / Scientific Question: For this county, how often has each rainy season been wetter, near normal or drier than the 1991–2020 normal when a given ocean driver phase was in place, and how far does that conditional frequency move away from the one-in-three climatological base rate?

5.3.1 Scientific Purpose & Context

Seasonal forecasts for Kenya are expressed as shifts in the odds of the three rainfall terciles, but county planners rarely see the historical evidence behind those shifts for their own county. Figure 3.2 supplies it. For every year in the record it places the county’s seasonal rainfall in a tercile of its own 1991–2020 distribution and the season’s ocean driver in a phase class, then cross-tabulates the two. The result is the empirical conditional frequency of a wetter, near-normal or drier season under each driver phase, shown as one stacked bar per phase against a dashed reference line at the one-in-three climatological base rate.

The two rainy seasons are shown side by side because they respond to different drivers. For the Short Rains (OND) the user can condition on the Pacific El Niño–Southern Oscillation, measured by the Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI), or on the Indian Ocean Dipole, measured by the Dipole Mode Index (DMI). For the Long Rains (MAM) the default driver is the Western-V sea surface temperature gradient in the western Pacific, with RONI as an alternative. Phases can be shown as three classes (positive, neutral, negative) or five (strong and moderate on each side of neutral), and as shares or as raw counts of years.

The figure turns a teleconnection into a plain statement a contingency planner can use, such as “in this county, seven of the last thirteen El Niño Short Rains seasons were in the wettest third”. It also shows where the signal is weak. Across Kenya’s 47 counties, OND seasons that coincided with El Niño fell in the wetter tercile 57 percent of the time when pooled, against 33 percent expected by chance, and at least half the time in 41 counties. The same conditioning gives no consistent shift for MAM. The Explorer surfaces both results so that county plans weight ENSO strongly for OND and treat it as uninformative for MAM.

5.3.2 Mathematical Formulation & Algorithmic Approach

Baseline Reference Period: Tercile bounds and driver standardization from the 1991–2020 WMO standard normal; matrix counts over the user-selected range (default 1981 to 2025)

Rainfall terciles. Terciles are assigned offline by the outlook builder. For each county and season, the 1991–2020 seasonal totals (county-mean CHIRPS v3 PTOT) form the baseline, and a county is skipped if fewer than 20 baseline years are available. The tercile bounds are the empirical 33.3rd and 66.7th percentiles computed with linear interpolation between order statistics (NumPy’s default method, equivalent to the Hyndman and Fan Type 7 estimator used elsewhere in the platform). Every year from 1981 to 2025 is then classed Drier if its total is at or below the lower bound, Wetter if it is strictly above the upper bound, and Near normal otherwise. Only the tercile bounds are used here; the decile bounds of Figure 3.1 are not.

Driver phase. For each season the selected driver is reduced to one value per year and standardized against its own 1991–2020 mean and sample standard deviation. For ENSO the value is the CPC RONI averaged over the concurrent season. For the IOD and the Western-V index the value is the mean of the available monthly values over October to December or March to May respectively (DMI from HadISST, falling back month by month to ERSST). The standardized value z is binned with symmetric thresholds: in three-class mode, positive phase if z ≥ +0.5, negative phase if z ≤ −0.5, and neutral otherwise; in five-class mode, strong positive if z ≥ +1.5, moderate positive if +0.5 ≤ z < +1.5, neutral if −0.5 < z < +0.5, moderate negative if −1.5 < z ≤ −0.5, and strong negative if z ≤ −1.5. Phase labels are El Niño and La Niña for RONI, positive and negative IOD for DMI, and high and low Western-V.

Contingency matrix. For the selected county and the years in the chosen range that have both a tercile and a driver value, the matrix counts years n_ij in driver class i and tercile j. Each row is normalised by its total to give the conditional frequency of each tercile within that phase, and the bars are drawn as these row shares (or as raw counts). Rows with fewer than five years are flagged in amber as small samples. No significance test or confidence interval is applied; the only reference is the 33.3 percent climatological base rate.

Insight summary. A text panel pools all positive-phase classes, reports the most frequent tercile and its share, and gives the mean and standard deviation of the seasonal rainfall anomaly in percent of the 1991–2020 mean for those years.

5.3.2.1 Methodological Rationale

Why a contingency table rather than a correlation. East African rainfall responds to ocean forcing asymmetrically. In OND, El Niño years shift the distribution strongly toward wet outcomes, while La Niña years produce a weaker and less consistent dry shift. A Pearson correlation between a driver index and seasonal rainfall compresses this into one number that is dominated by a few extreme years, such as 1997 and 2019. A contingency table keeps the asymmetry visible: each phase gets its own conditional distribution, and each cell is a count of real seasons that a planner can verify against memory.

Why terciles of each county’s own distribution. Seasonal rainfall in Kenya is strongly positively skewed and its level varies by an order of magnitude between counties, so a common millimetre threshold would be meaningless and a Gaussian tercile rule would misclassify dry seasons. Empirical terciles of each county’s own 1991–2020 record make every county’s categories equiprobable by construction, so a conditional frequency above one third is directly interpretable as a shift in the odds.

Why standardized driver thresholds. Standardizing each driver against its own 1991–2020 distribution puts RONI, DMI and Western-V on a common scale, so the same ±0.5 and ±1.5 standard deviation thresholds give comparable phase frequencies across drivers and seasons. The threshold is applied to the standardized value rather than to the raw index, so it differs from the conventional raw thresholds (0.5 °C for RONI, 0.4 °C for DMI) used for phase labels elsewhere in the Explorer. Because the 1991–2020 standard deviation of OND RONI is about 1.12, the OND El Niño class here requires roughly 0.56 °C, close to the conventional rule; for MAM the standard deviation is about 0.64, so a MAM El Niño class begins near 0.32 °C.

What the data show. Pooled over all 47 counties and 1981 to 2025, OND seasons in the El Niño class (13 years) were Wetter 57 percent, Near normal 25 percent and Drier 18 percent of the time; at the county level the share Wetter ranges from 31 to 69 percent, with a median of 54 percent, and reaches at least one half in 41 counties. OND La Niña seasons (18 years) were Drier 39 percent of the time, and only 10 counties reach a one-half Drier share, which confirms the asymmetry. For MAM, El Niño seasons were Wetter only 30 percent of the time and La Niña seasons Drier 37 percent, with almost no county above one half in either case: the Long Rains carry no consistent ENSO signal, in agreement with the hindcast skill reported in Table 2.3.

Empirical tercile bounds and class assignment. Bounds use linear interpolation between order statistics of the county’s 1991–2020 seasonal totals; counties with fewer than 20 baseline years are excluded.

5.3.4 Methodological Assumptions & Analytical Limitations

  • Sample sizes are small. Over 1981 to 2025 there are only 12 to 19 seasons in each three-class phase per season, and as few as two to four in the strong classes of five-class mode. A single season moves a share by six to eight percentage points, and the sampling standard error of a share near one third is about 13 percentage points for 13 years. No significance test or confidence interval is shown, so modest departures from the dashed line should not be read as a signal.
  • Counts are pooled over the whole selected range, including the 15 years outside the 1991–2020 baseline. Trends in rainfall or in driver behaviour across 1981 to 2025, including the drying of the Long Rains and the warming of the Indian Ocean, are not removed.
  • The driver phase uses the concurrent season. This describes the historical association between rainfall and the ocean state during the season, not predictability from information available before the season starts; forecast skill is assessed separately in Table 2.3.
  • Phase thresholds are applied to standardized values, so the effective raw threshold depends on the 1991–2020 variability of each index and season (about 0.56 °C for OND RONI and 0.32 °C for MAM RONI). Phase classes here therefore differ slightly from the raw-threshold phase labels used in other figures.
  • Seasonal driver means for the IOD and Western-V are taken over whichever months are available, with no completeness check, so a season with a missing month is represented by fewer than three months.
  • Tercile boundaries treat a total exactly equal to the upper bound as Near normal, whereas Figure 3.1 classes such a total as Wetter. The difference affects at most a single boundary year per county.
  • ENSO, the IOD and the Western-V gradient are not independent; positive IOD events frequently co-occur with El Niño. The figure conditions on one driver at a time and cannot separate their contributions.
  • County rainfall is a county-mean CHIRPS v3 value; uncertainty is larger in the sparsely gauged northern and eastern counties.
  • County aggregations rely on official polygon boundaries from the UN OCHA Common Operational Datasets (COD-AB).
